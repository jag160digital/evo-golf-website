● Inside 100 yards, near Derby & Nottingham

What Is a Short Game Practice Area?

A short game practice area is ground set aside for the shots that finish holes rather than start them: chips, pitches, bunker shots and putts. Ours is at Ormonde Fields Golf Club in Codnor, Ripley, alongside the Trackman driving range, and it is where golfers who care about their scorecard spend the second half of a practice session.

It is also, by some margin, the most neglected facility on any golf site in the country. Walk past a range at six on a summer evening near Derby or Nottingham and every bay is full of drivers. Walk past the chipping green and there will be one person on it, and they will be the one who has taken four shots off their handicap this year.

How Many Shots Actually Happen Inside 100 Yards?

For most club golfers, somewhere between half and two thirds of every round. Count them honestly on your next eighteen. A bogey golfer shooting around 90 will typically take 32 to 36 putts and add fifteen or so chips, pitches and bunker shots on top. That is well over half the card, played with clubs that get a fraction of the practice time.

Meanwhile there are fourteen tee shots, and maybe ten full iron shots. The maths is unkind to the way most people practise. Nobody wants to hear it, because hitting drivers is enjoyable and hitting forty yard pitches is not, but the shots are where the shots are.

Why Does the Short Game Decide Your Handicap?

Because it is where the big numbers come from and where they get rescued. A missed green is a par for the golfer who can chip and a double for the golfer who cannot, and over eighteen holes you will miss between eight and twelve greens. Improving your long game moves the misses closer. Improving your short game turns those misses into fours instead of sixes, and it does it far faster.

It is also the part of golf least dependent on physical ability, which is why it rewards practice so reliably. You do not need speed, flexibility or a new driver. You need repetitions and honest feedback, both of which are free once you are here. If you want the technique checked first, that is a lesson with an Advanced PGA Professional.

● Varied lies and a practice bunker

Why Does the Short Game Area Matter So Much?

Lower scores are built around the greens. Our short game area offers a variety of different lies, along with a practice bunker, allowing you to develop every aspect of your short game in realistic conditions.

The practice bunker is the part golfers travel for. Bunker play is the one shot in golf you cannot rehearse on a driving range, which is why so many amateurs stand in sand with no idea what they are supposed to do with the club. Half an hour in a real bunker, with a coach or without one, usually removes the fear completely.

The putting green does the other half of the job. Roughly forty per cent of the shots in a typical round are putts, and almost all of the ones that matter are inside ten feet. A green you can practise start line and speed on, from every angle and every slope, is worth more to your handicap than any club in the bag.

What Sort of Lies Will I Get?

Tight ones, fluffy ones, uphill, downhill and the awkward half-sitting-down lie that nobody practises and everybody faces. That variety is the point. A perfect lie teaches you one shot. A varied short game area teaches you to read the lie first and choose the shot second, which is what actually happens on the course.

If you want the technique checked before you build the repetitions, short game work is covered in a standard 1:1 golf lesson, and wedge distance control can be measured on Trackman in the teaching bay when the weather is against you.

● Four skills, one area

What Can You Practise in the Short Game Area?

Everything from a hundred yards in, on the same patch of ground, without moving your bag more than fifty paces.

Putting

The largest single line on your scorecard. Work on start line, speed control and the three to six footers that decide whether a good round survives the back nine. Bring a couple of tees and a coin.

Chipping

Low running shots from just off the surface, where most amateurs lose a shot a round through indecision more than technique. Practise landing spots rather than swings and the results arrive quickly.

Pitching

The awkward thirty to eighty yard range, which nobody enjoys and everybody faces. Distance control here is the difference between a tap-in and a twenty footer, and it responds enormously well to structured practice.

Bunker Play

The shot most golfers fear and almost nobody practises, largely because bunkers are unavailable on a driving range. Half an hour with a coach usually removes the fear entirely, because the technique is unusual rather than difficult.

● Where handicaps fall

Is Short Game Practice the Fastest Way to Lower Your Handicap?

For nearly every golfer above a five handicap, yes. A well used short game practice area pays back faster than anything else on a golf site, because the skills are simple, the repetitions are quick and the improvement transfers directly to the card. You can hit sixty chips in twenty minutes. You cannot do that with a driver, and it would not help if you could.

There is a reason coaches keep saying it and golfers keep ignoring it. Long game improvement is visible and satisfying. Short game improvement is quiet and turns up as a lower number on the scorecard three weeks later. One of those feels better in the moment and the other one actually works.

● A practice routine that works

How Should I Practise My Short Game?

An hour, split into four. This is roughly what we set golfers on the monthly programme, and it beats aimless chipping by a distance.

1

Fifteen minutes on short putts

Three to six feet, from every angle around one hole, and do not leave until you hole ten in a row. These are the putts that quietly cost two shots a round, and holing out under a small amount of self-imposed pressure is a skill in itself.

2

Fifteen minutes on speed

Long putts from thirty to fifty feet, ignoring the line entirely and judging only whether the ball finishes within a club length. Three putts come from poor speed far more often than from poor reading.

3

Fifteen minutes chipping to a landing spot

Put a towel or a tee where you want the ball to land, then chip to it rather than to the hole. Chipping is a landing spot and a roll-out ratio, and separating the two makes club selection obvious rather than instinctive.

4

Fifteen minutes on the shot you avoid

For most golfers that is a bunker shot or a forty yard pitch. Practise the one you dread, because it is the one costing you strokes. If you cannot get out of a bunker at all, book an hour with a coach before you spend a summer digging.

5

Finish with a test, not a drill

Nine random shots, one attempt each, scored up and down or not. Random beats blocked practice because golf is random. Record the score and try to beat it next week, the same way you would on the Trackman range.

● Putting, in more detail

What Is the Best Way to Practise Putting?

Separate Line From Speed

Almost every putting problem is one or the other, and practising them together means you learn neither. Spend one session on start line with a gate of two tees, and another on speed with no target at all beyond a rough distance. Golfers who do this for a month usually find the three putts disappear before the holed putts increase.

Practise the Length You Actually Face

Nobody has ever three putted from four feet, and nobody holes many from forty. The scoring putts are between three and ten feet, and that is where the practice time should sit. Watch a busy putting green and count how many people are lagging fifty footers because it is more pleasant than missing short ones.

Use Pressure, Even Artificial Pressure

Ten in a row from four feet, starting again from zero on a miss, is a genuinely uncomfortable drill by the seventh. That discomfort is the point. Practice without consequence teaches you to putt without consequence, which is not the situation you are training for.

Get the Basics Checked Once

Aim, eye position and face angle at impact are worth a single lesson, because they are almost impossible to self-diagnose and they undermine everything else. A short game session in the teaching bay or out on the green with a coach settles it, and golfers on the monthly coaching programme get it reviewed as a matter of routine.
